The Westminster Tilt, 1511

WESTMINSTER · 12–13 FEB 1511
FOR THE BIRTH OF A PRINCE

Four challengers have ridden in from the realm of Cuere Noble. Take up a lance against one of them, three courses down the tilt, and let the herald keep score.

Steer the red ring onto his helm

Choose your challenger

You ride as one of the answerers. Each challenger ran under an allegorical name.

How to ride

You steer the tip of your lance

  1. Your horse charges by itself. Just before you meet, a close-up of your opponent opens with your lance tip on it as a red ring.
  2. Hold the ↑ and ↓ arrow keys, or move the mouse up and down,Drag your finger up and down anywhere on the picture to steer the ring.
  3. The ring bobs with the gallop, so keep correcting. You strike wherever it is at the moment you meet.

Helm +2 · Body +1 · Saddle −1 · Tilt −2

Course 1

The herald’s verdict

Controls: hold ↑ / ↓ or move the mousedrag up and down on the picture to steer your lance tip (the red ring) onto the helm. It bobs with the gallop, so keep correcting until you meet.

The herald’s cheque

  • Top line: a stroke above it is a hit on the head, below it a hit on the body.
  • Middle line: lances broken.
  • Bottom line: faults.

This layout follows a herald’s cheque of 1520, from the jousts at the Field of Cloth of Gold, as described by the Society of Antiquaries. We use it here for 1511.

What happened at Westminster

On New Year’s Day 1511 Queen Katherine gave birth to a son, Henry, Duke of Cornwall. Henry VIII answered with the most lavish tournament of his reign, held at Westminster on 12 and 13 February. [challenge]

The challenge was framed as a fiction: four knights from the realm of Cuere Noble come “to accomplish certain feates of Armes” for the birth of a young prince. They were Sir William Courtenay as Bon Vouloir, Sir Edward Neville as Joyeulx Penser, Sir Thomas Knyvet as Vaillant Desir, and the king himself as Cueur Loyal. Fabrics carried the initials H and K. [challenge]

The day is painted on the Westminster Tournament Roll: 36 vellum membranes, almost 60 feet long, now at the College of Arms. In its joust scene the challengers stand at one end, the answerers at the other, and the queen watches from a pavilion. A royal trumpeter, John Blanke, appears twice, the earliest known portrait of a named Black person in Britain. [roll]

The Roll flatters the king. It shows Henry shattering his lance on his opponent’s helm, the best possible stroke, but he never actually scored that hit. [medievalists]

The celebration did not last. The prince died on 22 February 1511, aged 52 days. [prince]

How a joust was scored

English jousts followed rules set down in 1466 by John Tiptoft, Earl of Worcester, as Constable of England. [manuscript] [text]

  • A spear broken between the saddle and the helm counts 1; broken on the helm, 2; broken so as to bear the rider out of the saddle, 3.
  • A spear broken on the saddle is disallowed one. Striking the tilt costs two, a second strike three, and three strikes lose the prize.
  • Striking the horse, or a man whose back is turned, loses the prize outright.
  • For the prize, unhorsing ranks first, then meeting coronel to coronel twice, then three strikes in the sight of the helm, then most spears broken.

What we simplified

  • A bout here is three courses. Riders’ colours, devices and skill are invented for the game.
  • Your aim only moves up and down, and the target zones are tidier than real armour.
  • Meeting coronel to coronel is left out.
