Why longitude was the problem

On 22 October 1707 a British fleet coming home from Gibraltar ran onto the rocks of the Scilly Isles at night. Four ships and between 1,400 and 2,000 men were lost. The fleet believed the Channel was open ahead. The disaster came to stand for the great unsolved problem of the sea: no one could tell how far east or west they were.

Latitude is easy: the height of the sun at noon gives it. Longitude needs the time. The Earth turns 15° an hour, so if you know what time it is at Greenwich when the sun is highest where you are, the difference gives your longitude: four minutes of time for every degree. But no clock of the day could keep time on a rolling ship. So navigators used dead reckoning: compass course, plus speed from a knotted log line timed with a sandglass, plus hours sailed. Currents, leeway and a badly marked log made the error grow every day.

In 1714 Parliament offered £20,000 for a method good to half a degree. The carpenter and clockmaker John Harrison spent most of his life on it. His fourth clock, H4, a large watch, lost just 5 seconds, after allowing for its known daily rate, on the 81-day voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62, and passed a second trial to Barbados in 1764.

Before the clock, the safest way to find an island was to run down the latitude: get to the island’s latitude well to the east of it, then sail due west along that line until it came up over the horizon.

What this version simplifies

  • Each voyage has hidden conditions: a current that drifts from day to day, a log line knotted a little long or short, and a small steady steering error. Winds only change your speed, not where you can go.
  • The noon sun sight is good to a couple of miles of latitude, unless the sky is overcast. The clock fix is good to a mile or two of longitude, about what H4 achieved.
  • Land is seen within about 14 miles in daylight. Your destination can be seen from further off, by how high it stands. In the dark you see nothing until you hit it.
  • The shaded band is drawn wide enough to hold your true position about nine times in ten.
  • Coastlines are from Natural Earth (public domain), with the Scilly Isles added by hand. Voyages begin a little offshore.
